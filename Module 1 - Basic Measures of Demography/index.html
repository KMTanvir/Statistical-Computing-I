Masculinity Proportion

The Masculinity Proportion answers a simple question: what share of the total population is male?

MP  =  M ÷ (M + F)  ×  100

where M and F are the male and female mid year population counts. The result is a percentage, usually close to 50.

Compute it in R

# Total male and female counts
M <- sum(pop$male)
F <- sum(pop$female)

# Masculinity Proportion
MP <- M / (M + F) * 100
round(MP, 2)
#> [1] 49.58
How to read it: in Pabna 2022, roughly 49.58% of the population is male. The remaining 50.42% is female. The slight female majority is typical in Bangladesh and reflects female longevity in adult and older age groups.

If you ran MP <- M / (M + F) without the * 100 at the end, what would the value look like?

Sex Ratio and Excess of Males

Sex Ratio (SR)

The Sex Ratio asks the same question in a different way: how many males are there for every 100 females?

SR  =  M ÷ F  ×  100 
SR <- M / F * 100
round(SR, 2)
#> [1] 98.34

A Sex Ratio of 98.34 means there are about 98 males for every 100 females in Pabna 2022. Values below 100 indicate a female majority, values above 100 indicate a male majority.

Excess or Deficit of Males

Sometimes we want the raw count gap, not a ratio. The Excess (or Deficit) of Males compares actual males to the number expected under a chosen target Sex Ratio. With a balanced target of 100, the gap is simply M minus F.

Excess of Males  =  M  −  (SRtarget ÷ 100) × F 
# With a balanced target Sex Ratio of 100
excess <- M - F
excess
#> [1] -22000

# With a custom target, say SR = 105
sr_target <- 105
excess_target <- M - (sr_target / 100) * F
excess_target
#> [1] -88100
How to read it: a value of −22,000 means Pabna has a deficit of 22,000 males relative to a perfectly balanced population. A positive value would indicate an excess.

Quick conversion between MP and SR

The two measures are connected by simple algebra:

SR  =  MP ÷ (100  −  MP)  ×  100
MP  =  SR ÷ (100  +  SR)  ×  100

Aged Child Ratio

The Aged Child Ratio (ACR) measures how many older people there are for every 100 children. It is a quick lens on the age structure of a population.

ACR  =  P65+ ÷ P0 to 14  ×  100

where P65+ is the population aged 65 and over, and P0 to 14 is the population aged 0 to 14.

Compute it in R

Our age groups are 5 year bands. The first three rows cover ages 0 to 14, and the last four rows cover ages 65 and over.

# Total for each age group (both sexes combined)
pop$total <- pop$male + pop$female

# Population by broad age band
P_0_14  <- sum(pop$total[1:3])     # rows 1, 2, 3 = ages 0 to 14
P_15_64 <- sum(pop$total[4:13])    # rows 4 to 13 = ages 15 to 64
P_65    <- sum(pop$total[14:17])   # rows 14 to 17 = ages 65 and over

c("0-14" = P_0_14, "15-64" = P_15_64, "65+" = P_65)
#>    0-14   15-64     65+ 
#>  793900 1685200  144200

ACR <- P_65 / P_0_14 * 100
round(ACR, 2)
#> [1] 18.16
How to read it: for every 100 children aged 0 to 14 in Pabna, there are about 18 older adults aged 65 and over. A low value points to a young population; a high value points to an ageing one. Japan, for comparison, has an ACR above 250.
Why row indices? Because our data frame is already sorted by age, integer subsetting with [1:3], [4:13], [14:17] is the cleanest way to grab broad age bands. If the row order ever changed, you would switch to logical subsetting like pop$age_group %in% c("0-4","5-9","10-14") instead.

Age Dependency Ratios

Dependency ratios compare the population that is not economically active (children and the elderly) to the working age population. They are the demographer's quick proxy for the burden on workers.

Three flavours

Total Dependency Ratio (TDR)  =  (P0 to 14 + P65+) ÷ P15 to 64  ×  100

Child Dependency Ratio (CDR)  =  P0 to 14 ÷ P15 to 64  ×  100

Old Age Dependency Ratio (OADR)  =  P65+ ÷ P15 to 64  ×  100

Notice that TDR equals CDR plus OADR, by construction.

All three in R, in one go

TDR  <- (P_0_14 + P_65) / P_15_64 * 100
CDR  <- P_0_14            / P_15_64 * 100
OADR <- P_65              / P_15_64 * 100

round(c(Total = TDR, Child = CDR, OldAge = OADR), 2)
#>  Total  Child OldAge 
#>  55.67  47.11   8.56

Results at a glance

MeasureFormulaPabna 2022
Total Dependency(P0-14 + P65+) / P15-64 × 10055.67
Child DependencyP0-14 / P15-64 × 10047.11
Old Age DependencyP65+ / P15-64 × 1008.56
How to read TDR = 55.67: for every 100 working age persons in Pabna, there are about 56 dependants. Of those, around 47 are children and only 9 are elderly. This is the classic profile of a country still mid way through its demographic transition: most of the dependency burden still sits on the child side.

Population Pyramid in R

The single chart that summarises a population at a glance.

A population pyramid is a back to back horizontal bar chart: each age band has one bar going left for males and one bar going right for females. In base R you can build it in a few lines using barplot() by plotting the male counts as negative numbers.

The code, line by line

op <- par(no.readonly = TRUE)         # 1. save current par so we can restore it later
par(mar = c(4, 5, 3, 2))              # 2. widen the left margin to fit the age labels

mat <- rbind(-pop$male, pop$female)     # 3. one matrix, males negated so they fall to the left of 0

barplot(mat,
        horiz     = TRUE,                  # 4. horizontal bars instead of vertical
        names.arg = pop$age_group,         # 5. age band label next to each row, youngest at the bottom
        beside    = FALSE,                 # 6. stack the two rows so each age band is one paired bar
        space     = 0,                     # 7. no gap between adjacent age bands, classic pyramid look
        col       = c("#2563eb", "#f472b6"),  # 8. blue for male, pink for female
        xlab      = "Population (males left, females right)",   # 9. x axis title
        main      = "Pabna District, 2022",  # 10. plot title
        las       = 1,                      # 11. age labels printed horizontally, not rotated
        xlim      = c(-160000, 160000),     # 12. symmetric x range so the pyramid is centred
        axes      = FALSE)                  # 13. suppress the default x axis, we redraw it below

ticks <- seq(-150000, 150000, by = 50000)   # 14. tick positions every 50,000
axis(1, at = ticks, labels = abs(ticks))   # 15. redraw x axis with positive labels on both sides

legend("topright", legend = c("Male", "Female"),
       fill = c("#2563eb", "#f472b6"),
       bty  = "n")                          # 16. legend in the top right, no border box

par(op)                                    # 17. restore the original par settings
Reading order of the rows: barplot() draws horizontal bars from the bottom up in the order the columns appear. Because our data frame is sorted youngest first, age band 0 to 4 ends up at the bottom and 80+ at the top, the standard pyramid orientation.

What the chart looks like

What to look for in a pyramid. A wide base with a narrow top means a young, growing population. A narrow base with a wide top means an ageing population. Sudden indentations point to past events such as famines, wars, or migration shocks. Asymmetric bars at older ages reflect female longevity.
