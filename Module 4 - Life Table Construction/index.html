Types of Life Tables and the Canonical Columns

A life table is a statistical tool that summarises mortality, survival, and life expectancy for a population, age band by age band.

Two splits, four flavours

Cohort (Generation) Life Table. Follows one cohort, all individuals born in the same period, from birth until everyone has died. Accurate but slow; the table for people born in 1950 is only complete around 2050.

Period (Current) Life Table. Uses the age specific mortality rates observed in a single recent period, assuming they remain constant. This is the standard table used in demography because it is timely.
Complete vs Abridged. A complete life table uses single year age intervals (0, 1, 2, …, 100+). An abridged life table uses wider bands (0 to 4, 5 to 9, …, 85+). We will build an abridged period table, the practical choice for most real datasets.

The eight canonical columns

ColumnNameMeaning
xAgeStarting age of the interval (0, 5, 10, ...).
nInterval widthYears in the band (5 for an abridged table, with an open last band).
nMxAge specific death rateInput. Deaths per person year in the interval, often reported per 1,000.
nqxProbability of dyingChance a person alive at age x dies before age x+n.
npxProbability of surviving1 − nqx. The complement.
lxSurvivors at exact age xNumber still alive at exact age x out of l0 born. The radix l0 is usually 100,000.
ndxDeaths in the intervalNumber who die between x and x+n. ndx = lx × nqx.
nLxPerson years livedTotal years lived in the interval by survivors and decedents combined.
TxCumulative person yearsSum of nLx from age x onwards. Lives still to be lived from age x.
exLife expectancy at age xAverage remaining years for a person alive at exact age x. ex = Tx / lx.
Why eight columns? Each one answers a different question. nqx is "how likely am I to die in the next 5 years?", lx is "how many of us are left?", ex is "how many years can I expect to live?". The next five slides build them one by one.

Our Working Dataset

Dhaka District 2024, illustrative age specific mortality rates.

The only input we need is a vector of age specific death rates, nMx, one per age band. We will use 10 bands: 9 closed bands of width 5 years, and one open band 45+. The radix is the standard l0 = 100,000.

# Dhaka District 2024, abridged age specific death rates (per person year)
age      <- c(0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45)
n        <- c(5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, NA)   # last band is open
nMx      <- c(0.0060, 0.0008, 0.0008,
            0.0012, 0.0018, 0.0025,
            0.0035, 0.0050, 0.0075,
            0.0300)

lt <- data.frame(age, n, nMx)

l0 <- 100000      # standard radix

Why an open last interval? Mortality at very old ages is too volatile to band finely. The open interval absorbs every survivor from age 45 onwards. It needs special treatment for nqx (always 1) and nLx (lx / nMx), as we will see on Slides 4 and 6.

Probabilities of Dying and Surviving

The first computed column is nqx, the probability that a person alive at exact age x dies before reaching x+n. From mortality rates to probabilities, the Fergany method gives a clean exponential transform.

nqx  =  1  −  e−n × nMx

For the open last interval, nqx = 1 by construction, because everyone in the interval will eventually die in it.

Compute nqx and npx in R

# Fergany method for the closed bands
lt$nqx <- 1 - exp(-lt$n * lt$nMx)

# Override the open last band
last <- nrow(lt)
lt$nqx[last] <- 1

# Survival probability is just the complement
lt$npx <- 1 - lt$nqx

lt[, c("age", "nMx", "nqx", "npx")]

Survivors and Deaths

The survivors column lx tracks how many of the original radix are still alive at each exact age. Starting from l0 = 100,000, each next value is the previous one multiplied by the survival probability.

lx+n  =  lx  ×  npx
equivalently   lx  =  l0  ×  npx0  ×  npx1  ×  …  ×  npxx−n

Build lx with cumprod()

Vectorise it: the chain of products is exactly what cumprod() computes.

# Chain of survival probabilities, starting from l0
lt$lx <- l0 * cumprod(c(1, lt$npx[-last]))

# Deaths in each interval
lt$ndx <- lt$lx * lt$nqx

Person Years Lived in Each Interval

nLx is the total person years lived by everyone in the age band, survivors and decedents combined. It is the engine that converts numbers of people into life expectancy.

Two formulas, one for closed and one for open intervals

Closed band:   nLx  =  (n ÷ 2)  ×  (lx + lx+n)

Open last band:   nLx  =  lx ÷ nMx

The closed formula is the trapezoidal rule: average the survivors at the start and end of the band, then multiply by the band width. The open formula assumes a constant exponential mortality after age 45, so the total years lived equal lx divided by the rate.

Build nLx in R

lt$nLx <- NA_real_

# Closed bands: trapezoidal average
for (i in 1:(last - 1)) {
  lt$nLx[i] <- (lt$n[i] / 2) * (lt$lx[i] + lt$lx[i + 1])
}

# Open last band
lt$nLx[last] <- lt$lx[last] / lt$nMx[last]

lt[, c("age", "lx", "nLx")]

Why is the open nLx so large? 2.88 million person years for 86,458 survivors works out to about 33 years on average past age 45, which is exactly 1 / nMx = 1 / 0.030. The open interval absorbs all remaining lifetime.

Cumulative Person Years and Life Expectancy

Tx totals up the person years still to be lived from age x onwards. ex divides Tx by the survivors at that age, giving average remaining life.

Tx  =  nLx + nLx+n + nLx+2n + …

ex  =  Tx ÷ lx

Reverse cumulative sum in R

The natural R idiom is rev(cumsum(rev(x))): reverse the vector, take a forward cumulative sum, then reverse the result.

lt$Tx <- rev(cumsum(rev(lt$nLx)))
lt$ex <- lt$Tx / lt$lx

What the ex curve looks like

How to read it. Life expectancy at birth is e0 = 71.3 years. A person who has already reached age 25 has about e25 = 49.7 more years to live on average, so they are expected to live to about 75. Putting It Together

One R function that turns nMx and band widths into the full abridged life table.

The build_life_table() function

build_life_table <- function(age, n, nMx, l0 = 100000) {

  k <- length(nMx)

  # Fergany method for nqx, then override the open last band
  nqx <- 1 - exp(-n * nMx)
  nqx[k] <- 1
  npx <- 1 - nqx

  # Survivors and deaths
  lx  <- l0 * cumprod(c(1, npx[-k]))
  ndx <- lx * nqx

  # nLx, trapezoidal for closed and lx/nMx for the open band
  nLx <- numeric(k)
  for (i in 1:(k - 1)) {
    nLx[i] <- (n[i] / 2) * (lx[i] + lx[i + 1])
  }
  nLx[k] <- lx[k] / nMx[k]

  # Tx and ex
  Tx <- rev(cumsum(rev(nLx)))
  ex <- Tx / lx

  data.frame(age, n, nMx, nqx, npx, lx, ndx, nLx, Tx, ex)
}

# Apply to Dhaka 2024
dhaka_lt <- build_life_table(age, n, nMx)
