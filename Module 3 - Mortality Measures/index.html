Welcome to Module 3

Where mortality data comes from

Before any rate is computed, the numbers have to come from somewhere. There are three sources you will meet throughout this module.

  • Vital Registration System. The primary source. Births and deaths are recorded continuously by civil registration authorities. In high coverage countries this is the gold standard.
  • National Census. A periodic enumeration of the population. The standard source for the denominator (population at risk).
  • National Sample Survey. Survey based estimates used in countries where vital registration is incomplete, such as the Bangladesh Sample Vital Registration System (SVRS).

What you will be able to compute by the end of this lab

  • Crude Death Rate, the simplest mortality measure, with and without completeness adjustment
  • Neonatal, Infant, and Child Mortality Rates, the infant and child specific measures
  • Age Specific Death Rate, one rate per age band, and plot the mortality curve
  • Direct standardisation, the cleanest way to compare mortality across populations with different age structures
  • Indirect standardisation, the alternative when only total deaths and the age structure are known

Our Working Dataset

Mymensingh District 2024, plus a reference standard population we will use for age standardisation.

We will work with an illustrative mortality table for Mymensingh District in 2024, six broad age bands with deaths and mid year population in each. We will also keep two completeness constants for the registration system, and a small reference standard population we will need on Slide 6.

Create the data frame and the supporting constants

# Mymensingh District, mid year 2024 (illustrative figures)
age_group  <- c("0-14", "15-29", "30-44",
                 "45-59", "60-74", "75+")

deaths     <- c(560, 520, 1100, 2550, 9000, 18750)

population <- c(1400000, 1300000, 1100000,
                 850000,  600000,  250000)

mort <- data.frame(age_group, deaths, population)

# Completeness of registration (fractions)
C_d <- 0.88      # deaths registration is 88% complete
C_p <- 0.92      # census coverage is 92% complete

# Inputs for the infant and child mortality measures (Slide 4)
B            <- 125000   # total live births in 2024
deaths_neo   <- 1875     # deaths aged 0 to 28 days
deaths_inf   <- 3000     # deaths aged 0 to 12 months
deaths_child <- 1568     # deaths aged 1 to 4 years
P_1_4        <- 448000   # mid year population aged 1 to 4

# Reference standard population (per 100,000), used in Slide 6
std_pop <- c(28000, 25000, 20000, 15000, 8000, 4000)

# Standard age specific death rates (per 1000), used in Slide 8
M_std <- c(0.4, 0.5, 1.2, 3.5, 17.0, 80.0)
M_std_crude <- 5.56   # crude rate in the standard population (per 1000)

What the supporting constants are for

  • C_d, C_p, completeness fractions, used on Slide 3 to correct the CDR.
  • B, deaths_neo, deaths_inf, deaths_child, P_1_4, the inputs for the Neonatal, Infant, and Child Mortality Rates on Slide 4.

Why the reference standard population matters. A standard is just an arbitrary, fixed age distribution shared between the populations we want to compare. On Slide 6 we will apply each population's ASDR to this same standard, so any difference in the resulting rate is due to mortality, not to age structure.

Crude Death Rate

The Crude Death Rate is the simplest mortality measure: total deaths over total mid year population, expressed per 1,000 people.

CDR  =  D ÷ P  ×  1000

where D is the number of deaths during the year and P is the mid year total population.

Compute it in R

D <- sum(mort$deaths)        # 32,480 deaths in 2024
P <- sum(mort$population)    # 5,500,000 mid year population

CDR <- D / P * 1000
CDR
#> [1] 5.91

So Mymensingh recorded about 5.91 deaths per 1,000 people in 2024.

Why "crude"? Just like CBR, the CDR denominator includes everyone, regardless of age. Two populations with the same age specific mortality but very different age structures will report very different CDRs. The next two slides fix this in two different ways.

Adjusting CDR for Completeness

In many developing settings, the vital registration system misses some deaths and the census misses some people. When that happens, the raw CDR is biased. If we know the completeness of each, we can correct for it.

CDRcorrected  =  (D ÷ Cd) ÷ (P ÷ Cp)  ×  1000

where Cd is the fraction of true deaths that are registered, and Cp is the fraction of the true population that is counted. Both fractions sit between 0 and 1.

Equivalent shortcut

A little algebra gives a simpler form:

CDRcorrected  =  CDR  ×  Cp ÷ Cd

Compute both in R

# The full formula, inflate D and P separately
CDR_corr1 <- (D / C_d) / (P / C_p) * 1000
CDR_corr1
#> [1] 6.18

# The shortcut, scale the crude CDR
CDR_corr2 <- CDR * C_p / C_d
CDR_corr2
#> [1] 6.18
How to read it: the raw CDR of 5.91 understates mortality because deaths are missed at a higher rate than the census misses people. Correcting raises the estimate to 6.18 per 1,000.
When to apply the correction. Use the adjustment only when you have credible estimates of Cd and Cp, typically from a post enumeration survey or a sample registration system. Without those, the crude CDR is what you report, with a note on the registration system's known limitations.

Neonatal, Infant, and Child Mortality

These three measures isolate mortality at the very youngest ages, where deaths are highest and where most public health interventions are targeted. All three use live births in the denominator instead of population.

Neonatal Mortality Rate (NMR)

Deaths during the first 28 days of life per 1,000 live births.

NMR  =  N ÷ B  ×  1000

where N is neonatal deaths (0 to 28 days) and B is total live births in the same year.

NMR <- deaths_neo / B * 1000
NMR
#> [1] 15.00

Infant Mortality Rate (IMR)

Deaths during the first year of life per 1,000 live births. Subsumes NMR.

IMR  =  I ÷ B  ×  1000

where I is infant deaths (0 to 12 months).

IMR <- deaths_inf / B * 1000
IMR
#> [1] 24.00

Child Mortality Rate (CMR)

Deaths between exact ages 1 and 5 per 1,000 mid year population aged 1 to 4. Note that the denominator here is population, not live births, because by age 1 children are tracked through the census, not the birth register.

CMR  =  C ÷ P1 to 4  ×  1000

where C is deaths aged 1 to 4 and P1 to 4 is the mid year population in that age range.

CMR <- deaths_child / P_1_4 * 1000
CMR
#> [1] 3.50

Results at a glance

MeasureFormulaMymensingh 2024
NMRN / B × 100015.00
IMRI / B × 100024.00
CMRC / P1-4 × 10003.50
Why three separate measures? NMR captures conditions tied to birth (prematurity, birth asphyxia, infection at birth). The gap between IMR and NMR points to post neonatal causes such as diarrhoeal disease, acute respiratory infection, and undernutrition. CMR captures the residual under five mortality not driven by infancy. Tracking the three together separates problems with delivery care from problems with sanitation, vaccination, and child nutrition.

Age Specific Death Rate

ASDR breaks mortality down by age band. For each band, divide the deaths in the band by the mid year population in the band. The result is a vector of rates that describes the mortality profile of the population.

ASDRa  =  Da ÷ Pa  ×  1000

Compute the full ASDR vector

mort$ASDR <- mort$deaths / mort$population * 1000
mort[, c("age_group", "ASDR")]
#>   age_group  ASDR
#> 1      0-14  0.40
#> 2     15-29  0.40
#> 3     30-44  1.00
#> 4     45-59  3.00
#> 5     60-74 15.00
#> 6       75+ 75.00

Plot the mortality curve

plot(mort$ASDR, type = "o", pch = 19, col = "#dc2626",
     xaxt = "n", xlab = "Age group", ylab = "ASDR (per 1000)",
     main = "Mymensingh 2024, Age Specific Mortality")
axis(1, at = 1:6, labels = mort$age_group)

What the curve looks like

The classic J shape. Mortality is low and almost flat through young adulthood, then climbs steeply from middle age onwards. The 75+ rate is more than twenty times the rate at 60 to 74. If we want to compare populations fairly, we cannot ignore this age dependence, which is what brings us to standardisation.

CDR is a weighted average of ASDRs

The two summaries are tied together algebraically. The Crude Death Rate is the population weighted average of the age specific rates, where the weights are the share of the population in each age band.

CDR  =  Σ ASDRa  ×  (Pa ÷ P) 
# Verify the identity
weights <- mort$population / sum(mort$population)
sum(mort$ASDR * weights)
#> [1] 5.905   (matches the CDR computed on Slide 3)
Why this matters. Two populations can have the same ASDRs in every age band, but one is heavily skewed towards older ages while the other is skewed young. The age weighted average will be higher for the older population, even though the underlying mortality is identical. This is exactly why direct standardisation, coming up on Slide 7, replaces the population weights with a shared standard.

Direct Standardisation

Two populations with similar CDRs can have very different true mortality. The trick is to apply each population's age specific rates to one shared standard age structure, so age differences drop out of the comparison.

Age standardised rate  =  Σ (ASDRa  ×  Pstd,a)  ÷  Σ Pstd,a

The numerator is the deaths we would expect in the standard population if it had Mymensingh's mortality. Divide by the standard's total size and you get a comparable rate.

Compute it in R

# Expected deaths per standard person in each band
expected <- mort$ASDR * std_pop

# Age standardised death rate (per 1000)
ASR <- sum(expected) / sum(std_pop)
ASR
#> [1] 5.06

Why this rescues the comparison

Suppose Bogura District has a CDR very close to Mymensingh's, but a younger age structure. Applying the same standard to both populations strips out the age effect and shows which one really has higher mortality at every age.

MeasureMymensingh 2024Bogura 2024 (exercises)
Crude Death Rate5.915.81
Corrected CDR6.186.31
Age Standardised Rate5.068.38
What standardisation revealed. The crude rates suggested almost identical mortality. After standardising, Bogura's age standardised rate is about 65% higher than Mymensingh's, because Bogura's younger age structure was masking heavier mortality at every age band. You will work through this calculation for Bogura in the Exercises.
When direct standardisation is not possible. Direct standardisation needs reliable ASDRs for the study population. When those are not available, only total deaths and the age structure, we switch to the indirect method covered on Slide 8.

Indirect Standardisation

Direct standardisation needed each population's age specific death rates. When you only have the total death count and the age structure, the indirect method gives you a comparable rate by going in the opposite direction: apply a standard set of ASDRs to the study population.

The Standardised Mortality Ratio (SMR)

First compute the deaths we would expect in the study population if it had the standard's age specific mortality. Then divide observed deaths by expected.

Expected deaths  =  Σ (Mstd,a  ×  Pobs,a) ÷ 1000

SMR  =  Dobserved ÷ expected deaths

SMR is a unitless ratio. SMR = 1 means the study matches the standard, SMR > 1 means heavier mortality than the standard, SMR < 1 means lighter.

From SMR to an indirectly standardised rate

Multiplying SMR by the standard's crude rate gives a rate on the same per 1,000 scale as a CDR.

Indirectly standardised rate  =  SMR  ×  Mstd,crude

Compute it in R

# Total observed deaths in Mymensingh
D_obs <- sum(mort$deaths)         # 32,480

# Expected deaths if Mymensingh had the standard's ASDRs
expected <- sum(M_std * mort$population) / 1000
expected
#> [1] 35705

# Standardised Mortality Ratio
SMR <- D_obs / expected
SMR
#> [1] 0.910

# Indirectly standardised death rate (per 1000)
IDR <- SMR * M_std_crude
IDR
#> [1] 5.06
How to read it: SMR = 0.910 means Mymensingh has about 9% fewer deaths than the standard's rates would predict. Multiplying SMR by the standard's crude rate (5.56) gives an indirectly standardised rate of 5.06 per 1,000, exactly matching the direct standardisation result on Slide 7. Both methods agree on the same conclusion.

Direct or indirect, which to use?

Use direct whenUse indirect when
You have reliable age specific death rates for each population being compared. Only total deaths and the age structure are available for the study population.
Populations are large enough that ASDRs are stable. Some age bands have very few people, making their ASDRs unstable.
You want a clean rate comparable across many populations. You want a single summary ratio (SMR) against a reference.
