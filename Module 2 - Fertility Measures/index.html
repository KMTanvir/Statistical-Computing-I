Our Working Dataset

Khulna District 2024, an illustrative fertility table with everything we need.

We will work with a single tidy table for Khulna District in 2024. Numbers are realistic for a mid sized Bangladesh district but are illustrative, not official figures. The dataset has seven rows, one per 5 year reproductive age band, and the columns we will use across the rest of the module.

Create the data frame in R

# Khulna District, mid year 2024 (illustrative figures)
age_group  <- c("15-19", "20-24", "25-29", "30-34",
                 "35-39", "40-44", "45-49")

births     <- c(8000, 15000, 11000, 7000,
                 2200, 600,   100)

female_pop <- c(110000, 105000, 100000, 95000,
                 85000,  75000,  65000)

L5x        <- c(494000, 491000, 488000, 484500,
                 480000, 474000, 466000)  # from a life table, radix 100,000

fert <- data.frame(age_group, births, female_pop, L5x)

# Two more constants we will use later
total_pop <- 2400000     # Khulna mid year total population
p_female  <- 0.487        # proportion of births that are girls (Bangladesh average)

Crude Birth Rate and Monthly Birth Rate

The two simplest period rates. Both put total live births over a total population, the only difference is whether the window is a year or a single month.

Crude Birth Rate (CBR)

CBR  =  B ÷ P  ×  1000

where B is total live births in the year and P is mid year total population.

B <- sum(fert$births)            # 43,900 total births in 2024
CBR <- B / total_pop * 1000
round(CBR, 2)
#> [1] 18.29

So Khulna had about 18.29 live births per 1,000 people in 2024.

Why "crude"? The denominator P includes men, children, and post menopausal women, none of whom can give birth. The numerator and denominator do not match well, so the rate is sensitive to the age and sex structure of the population. GFR (Slide 4) is the first fix.

Monthly Birth Rate (MBR), annualised

If you only have one month of data, you scale it up by the days in a year to put it on the same footing as an annual rate.

MBR  =  (365 ÷ d)  ×  Bmonth ÷ Pmonth  ×  1000

where d is the number of days in that month, Bmonth the births during the month, and Pmonth the population during the month.

# Khulna, March 2024: 31 days, 3,720 live births recorded
B_month <- 3720
P_month <- 2400000
d       <- 31

MBR <- (365 / d) * B_month / P_month * 1000
round(MBR, 2)
#> [1] 18.25
Sanity check: CBR ≈ 18.29 and MBR ≈ 18.25. They are close because the monthly count we used (3,720) is close to the yearly average per month (43,900 / 12 ≈ 3,658). If the monthly count had been seasonally high or low, the annualised MBR would have drifted away from the annual CBR.

General Fertility Rate

GFR fixes the denominator problem with CBR by restricting it to women of reproductive age, the 15 to 49 band.

GFR  =  B ÷ Pf15 to 49  ×  1000

where B is total live births in the year and Pf15 to 49 is the mid year female population in the reproductive ages.

Compute it in R

Pf_15_49 <- sum(fert$female_pop)   # 635,000 women aged 15 to 49
GFR <- B / Pf_15_49 * 1000
round(GFR, 2)
#> [1] 69.13
How to read it: there were about 69.13 live births per 1,000 women aged 15 to 49 in Khulna in 2024. GFR is roughly 3.7 times CBR here, simply because the denominator shrank by about that factor.

The classic rule of thumb, GFR ≈ 4.5 × CBR

Across many populations, the ratio GFR to CBR stays remarkably stable, close to 4.5. It is not exact, but it is a useful sanity check and a quick way to estimate one rate from the other when the data is partial.

GFR  ≈  4.5  ×  CBR 
# Quick check on Khulna
4.5 * CBR
#> [1] 82.30

# Actual GFR computed from the data
GFR
#> [1] 69.13

The approximation predicts 82.3, the actual GFR is 69.1. The rule of thumb is on the right order of magnitude; the gap here mostly reflects Khulna's particular age and sex composition.

Age Specific Fertility Rate

ASFR pushes the denominator one step further. For each 5 year band, we divide births to women in that band by the female population in that band. The result is a vector of seven rates that together describe the fertility profile of the population.

ASFRa  =  Ba ÷ Pfa  ×  1000

Compute the full ASFR vector

fert$ASFR <- fert$births / fert$female_pop * 1000
round(fert[, c("age_group", "ASFR")], 2)
#>   age_group   ASFR
#> 1     15-19  72.73
#> 2     20-24 142.86
#> 3     25-29 110.00
#> 4     30-34  73.68
#> 5     35-39  25.88
#> 6     40-44   8.00
#> 7     45-49   1.54

Plot the ASFR curve

plot(fert$ASFR, type = "o", pch = 19, col = "#2563eb",
     xaxt = "n", xlab = "Age group", ylab = "ASFR (per 1000 women)",
     main = "Khulna 2024, Age Specific Fertility")
axis(1, at = 1:7, labels = fert$age_group)

What the curve looks like

The classic fertility curve. Rates rise from age 15, peak between 20 and 29, then fall sharply. In Bangladesh the peak typically sits at 20 to 24, the modal childbearing age. ASFR is the building block for every measure on the next four slides.

Total Fertility Rate

TFR sums the ASFRs across all reproductive ages to give the average number of children a woman would have over her lifetime if she experienced the current age specific rates throughout her reproductive years.

TFR  =  n  ×  Σ ASFRa ÷ 1000

where n is the width of each age band (5 here, for 5 year groups).

Compute it in R

n <- 5                                  # age band width
TFR <- n * sum(fert$ASFR) / 1000
round(TFR, 3)
#> [1] 2.173
How to read it: a TFR of 2.173 means a woman in Khulna, if she lived through her whole reproductive life experiencing 2024 age specific rates, would have about 2.17 live births on average. The widely cited replacement level TFR is around 2.10, so Khulna sits just above replacement.
Why we multiply by n. ASFR is births per 1,000 woman years. Each age band lasts n years, so a woman spends n woman years in it. Multiplying by n converts the per year rate into a per band count, and the sum across bands is a per lifetime count.

Gross Reproduction Rate

TFR counts all live births. GRR keeps only the female births, because only daughters can themselves go on to reproduce. It is the natural female only version of TFR.

GRR  =  TFR  ×  p

where p is the proportion of live births that are girls. In Bangladesh this is around 0.487, slightly under half because slightly more boys are born than girls.

Compute it in R

GRR <- TFR * p_female
round(GRR, 3)
#> [1] 1.058

Equivalent formulation, straight from ASFR

GRR2 <- n * sum(fert$ASFR * p_female) / 1000
round(GRR2, 3)
#> [1] 1.058
How to read it: a GRR of 1.058 means each Khulna woman would, on average, give birth to about 1.06 daughters over her reproductive lifetime, assuming no daughter ever dies before reaching the end of her own reproductive period.
Why GRR is always less than TFR. GRR = TFR × p with p < 1. The daughter only filter chops off the male births. GRR is always smaller than TFR, by a factor equal to the proportion of female births.

Net Reproduction Rate

GRR assumed every newborn daughter survives the full reproductive period. NRR drops that assumption by weighting each age band by the chance a woman is still alive in it, taken from a life table.

NRR  =  Σ ASFRa  ×  p  ×  (5Lx,a ÷ l0) ÷ 1000

where 5Lx,a is person years lived in age band a per radix l0, taken from a female life table. We will build life tables properly in Module 4; for now treat the L5x column as given.

Compute it in R

l0 <- 100000                                # life table radix

NRR <- sum(fert$ASFR * p_female * fert$L5x / l0) / 1000
round(NRR, 3)
#> [1] 1.034

Compare the three female only measures

MeasureFormulaKhulna 2024
TFRn × Σ ASFR / 10002.173

NRRΣ ASFR × p × 5Lx/l0 / 10001.034
How to read NRR = 1.034: on current age specific fertility and mortality, each woman in Khulna is replaced, in the next generation, by about 1.03 surviving daughters. Values above 1 indicate a population that is still slowly growing through natural increase. Below 1 indicates eventual decline.
